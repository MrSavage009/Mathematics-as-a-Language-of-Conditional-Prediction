# The Compliance Mask and the Closed Grammar

## A Foundational Case for Mathematics as a Language of Conditional Prediction

**Keywords:** mathematics pedagogy, conditional prediction, modeling-first instruction, mathematical literacy, curriculum design

---

### Abstract

Mathematics is taught as a **closed grammar**: a system of manipulation rules applied to an unknown \( x \), with the purpose of the grammar deferred to a later date that never arrives. This paper argues that the pedagogy is **epistemically incomplete, ontologically partial, and diagnostically self-concealing**. It is incomplete because it teaches syntax before semantics. It is partial because it treats a space of relationships as a point to be extracted. It is self-concealing because its failure is visible only in the students who refuse to comply, while the students who comply without understanding produce the same output as the students who understand. The mask is **epistemic before it is demographic**: compliance and understanding are output-indistinguishable from the outside. The gender difference in compliance is one illustration of a more general problem, not the problem itself.

The paper reconstructs mathematics pedagogy as a language of **conditional prediction**: every statement is a conditional, every equation is a map of a relationship, and the use case precedes the syntax. The reconstruction is not a motivational supplement. It is a **schema** for what counts as a complete mathematical statement: condition, prediction, relationship, interpretation, failure. The five parts are constitutive, not decorative. A statement that gives the relationship but not the conditions is incomplete; a statement that gives the prediction but not the interpretation is incomplete. What is new here is not the diagnosis — which overlaps with modeling-first, functions-first, and realistic-mathematics traditions — but a **unified grammar** that makes those traditions operational in a single sentence form.

---

### 1. Introduction

A student asks: "Why are we solving for \( x \)?"

The teacher answers: "You'll need it later."

This exchange is the whole problem in miniature. The student has asked for the **use case** — the reason the language exists — and the teacher has offered a **deferral**. The reason will arrive later, in a future course, in a future application, in a future that the student is asked to trust without evidence. Some students accept the deferral. Others do not. The ones who do not often leave mathematics entirely. The ones who do often comply without understanding. And the classroom, looking at the completed problem sets of the compliant students, concludes that the pedagogy works.

This paper argues that the pedagogy does not work. It **sorts**. It rewards compliance in the absence of understanding, and it punishes the demand for reasons. It produces students who can manipulate symbols but cannot say what the symbols are for. And it hides this failure behind a **compliance mask**: the visible success of the students who defer the question "why?" conceals the invisible failure of the students who comply without understanding.

By "the current pedagogy" I mean the dominant sequence in secondary mathematics: introduce a procedure, work examples, assign exercises, assess by correct application. This sequence is not universal — it is displaced in modeling-first and inquiry-based curricula — but it remains the default in most standardized-testing jurisdictions, and it is the sequence that produces the compliance mask described in §2.

A **closed grammar**, in this paper's usage, is a system of manipulation rules applied to an unknown \( x \), with the purpose of the grammar deferred to a later date that never arrives. The grammar is closed because it does not admit the question "why?" as part of itself. The question is treated as external — a question about motivation, or application, or "relevance" — rather than as internal to what a complete mathematical statement is. A **complete** mathematical statement, in the alternative grammar this paper proposes, names its conditions, its prediction, its relationship, its interpretation, and its failure modes. The closed grammar names only the relationship, and treats the other four as optional. The "closed grammar" and the "point-first grammar" described in §3 are the same thing under two names: closed because it excludes the other four components, point-first because it treats the solution as a point.

The paper's thesis is that mathematics should be taught as a **language of conditional prediction** — a grammar in which every statement names its conditions, every equation describes a relationship, and every use case precedes its syntax. This is not a tweak to the existing curriculum. It is a change in what mathematics is taught to **be**.

The paper's diagnosis is not new. It overlaps with the modeling-first tradition (COMAP), Realistic Mathematics Education (Freudenthal 1973/1991), the functions-first curricula that have displaced "solve for \( x \)" in many jurisdictions, and the work of Boaler (2016) on mathematics anxiety and pedagogical reform. What this paper adds is not the diagnosis but a **unified grammar** that makes those traditions operational in a single sentence form: condition, prediction, relationship, interpretation, failure. The six worked examples in §5 are a demonstration that the grammar generalizes, not a claim that the underlying pedagogy is novel.

The paper proceeds as follows. §2 diagnoses the compliance mask — why the pedagogy survives its own failure, and why the mask is epistemic before it is demographic. §3 identifies the ontological error — point-thinking about a space — and states the subsumption relation between the two. §4 proposes the alternative grammar as a schema, not a template. §5 demonstrates the grammar across six domains, with the hard case (confidence intervals) last. §6 states what changes at the foundational level, with concrete examples of assessment and teacher training. §7 addresses objections. §8 concludes with a research program.

The grammar as stated applies to mathematics that describes or predicts the world. It does not claim to reconstruct pure mathematics, where the "conditions" are axiomatic and the "interpretation" is internal to the formal system. The scope claim is stated in full in §4.5.

### 2. The Compliance Mask

#### 2.1 The mask is epistemic before it is demographic

The pedagogy produces students who can execute the method without understanding why it exists. Their visible success — completed problem sets, correct answers, passing grades — conceals the failure. The mask is not that the students are lying; it is that **the output of compliance and the output of understanding are indistinguishable from the outside**. A teacher cannot tell them apart from the paper alone.

This is the paper's central diagnostic claim, and it is a claim about assessment, not about gender. The mask is epistemic: it is a feature of what the pedagogy produces, not a feature of who produces it. The gender difference, discussed in §2.3, is one illustration of how the mask operates. It is not the mask itself.

Understanding becomes visible when the student can state the conditions under which the method applies, interpret the relationship it describes, and identify when the model fails — tasks that cannot be performed by compliance alone. The current pedagogy does not ask for any of these, so the mask is never lifted.

#### 2.2 Three costs of the mask

The mask imposes three costs.

**First, it produces false success.** A classroom in which most students complete the exercises looks like a classroom in which most students understand. The mask makes the two indistinguishable. The teacher cannot tell the student who understands from the student who complies, because both produce the same output.

**Second, it produces delayed failure.** The student who complies without understanding often hits a wall later — in calculus, in statistics, in any context where the method must be **chosen** rather than **applied**. The student who disengaged early may never reach that wall because they left mathematics entirely. The student who complied may reach it and discover that they have been executing a language they never learned to speak. The mask does not prevent failure; it **postpones** it to a point where it is harder to diagnose and more costly to repair.

**Third, it distorts the diagnosis.** When we ask "why are students disengaged in mathematics?", the answer often given is that they need relevance, competition, or movement. When we ask "why are some students underrepresented at the top of mathematics?", the answer often given is that they lack confidence or face stereotype threat. Both diagnoses are partly right. But both miss the common root: **the pedagogy does not state its purpose, and students respond to that absence in different ways.** Some leave. Some stay and comply. Neither learns why the language exists.

#### 2.3 Why the pedagogy survives: the mask and its alternatives

The paper claims the mask is **one reason** the pedagogy has survived. It is not the only reason. Institutional inertia, textbook lock-in, teacher training that reproduces the pedagogy teachers received, and standardized testing that rewards extraction speed are all plausible and probably jointly sufficient. The mask is not the sole cause; it is the mechanism by which the pedagogy's failure is **camouflaged** from the inside.

The mask matters because it makes the pedagogy look like it works. A pedagogy that produced visible failure in everyone would be corrected. A pedagogy that produces visible success in some and visible failure in others can be attributed to the students rather than to the method. The mask allows the method to escape scrutiny.

The gender difference in compliance is one illustration of this mechanism, not the mechanism itself. On average, with large overlap and many exceptions, boys are more likely to disengage when the purpose of a task is not visible, and girls are more likely to comply without understanding it.¹ If the gender claim is wrong, the paper's diagnostic claim survives: the mask operates on anyone who complies without understanding, regardless of gender.

¹ The trait literature (Costa, Terracciano & McCrae 2001; Schmitt et al. 2008) and the mathematics-education literature (Ellis, Fosdick & Rasmussen 2016; Steele; Spencer, Steele & Quinn) offer competing accounts of this difference. The paper's claim is orthogonal to all of them.

#### 2.4 What removing the mask would require

Removing the mask means making understanding visible in **everyone** — not just in the students who refuse to comply, and not just in the students who comply. It means designing a pedagogy in which the question "why?" is answered in the first sentence, so that neither compliance nor refusal is necessary.

Understanding becomes visible when the student can state the conditions under which the method applies, interpret the relationship it describes, and identify when the model fails. These are the tasks the current pedagogy does not ask for. They are the tasks the alternative grammar in §4 is designed to require.

### 3. The Ontological Error: Point-Thinking About a Space

#### 3.1 Two pedagogies, defined

A pedagogy is **point-first** when it presents the unknown as the protagonist, the task as isolation, and the solution as a single value extracted from a relationship. A pedagogy is **space-first** when it presents the relationship as the protagonist, the task as description, and the solution as a characterization of the space the relationship defines — its features, its boundaries, its regions, and its behavior under change.

The current pedagogy teaches mathematics as a point-first grammar. The sentence form is:

> "Solve for \( x \): \( 3x + 7 = 22 \)."

The unknown is the protagonist. The task is to isolate it. The grammar assumes that the unknown is a point — a single value hidden inside the equation — and that the task of mathematics is to find it.

This grammar is not wrong for every situation. When the question is "at what time does the ball land?", the answer is a point, and the point-first grammar finds it. But most real-world situations are not points. They are **regions, trade-offs, equilibria, and trajectories**. The point-first grammar has no vocabulary for these. It can find a root, but it cannot describe the shape of the relationship whose root it found.

#### 3.2 The subsumption relation

Point-thinking is not wrong; it is a **special case** of space-thinking. When the space is a single point — when there is one solution and the conditions are fixed — the grammar of extraction finds it. The error is not teaching extraction; it is teaching **only** extraction, so that students have no vocabulary for the cases where the solution is a region, a trade-off, an equilibrium, or a trajectory.

This is a stronger claim than "point-thinking is wrong," because it is defensible and it explains what the alternative pedagogy is doing. The alternative is not a replacement of extraction; it is the **larger grammar** of which extraction is one operation among several.

#### 3.3 Mathematics as cartography

The alternative is to teach mathematics as a **cartography of spaces**. The sentence form is:

> "A relationship is described by \( y = x^2 - 5x + 6 \). Under the condition that \( x \) represents time in seconds, the relationship crosses zero at \( x = 2 \) and \( x = 3 \). The minimum occurs at \( x = 2.5 \). The discriminant is positive, so the relationship has two real roots and a region of negativity between them."

The relationship is the protagonist. The equation is a **map** of the relationship, and the "solution" is a **description of the map's features**: its roots, its vertex, its symmetry, its regions of positivity and negativity. The algebra is a tool for drawing the map, not a machine for extracting a point.

This is a claim about what equations are. An equation is a description of a relationship under conditions. The quadratic formula is a tool for characterizing a parabola. The discriminant is a topological invariant that tells you whether the parabola crosses the axis. The vertex is a critical point where the gradient vanishes. The roots are boundary points where the relationship changes sign. None of these are "the answer." They are **features of a space**.

#### 3.4 Natural law speaks in spaces

The point-first ontology is not just pedagogically ineffective; it is **partial about the world**. Natural law does not solve for points. It operates through:

- **Variational principles:** nature minimizes action, maximizes entropy, minimizes free energy. The "solution" is a **path** — the trajectory that extremizes a functional.
- **Gradients and flows:** heat flows from hot to cold, water flows downhill, populations follow resource gradients. The "solution" is a **direction** — the gradient of a potential.
- **Equilibria and attractors:** a pendulum settles at the bottom, a market clears, an ecosystem reaches a climax state. The "solution" is an **attractor** — a region of phase space toward which trajectories converge.
- **Constraints and feasible sets:** a crystal forms only within thermodynamic bounds, a species survives only within an ecological niche. The "solution" is a **feasible region** — the set of states that satisfy the constraints.
- **Symmetries and invariants:** Noether's theorem connects symmetries to conservation laws. The "solution" is an **invariance** — what remains unchanged under transformation.

These five patterns are illustrative, not exhaustive. They are enough to show that the syntax of nature is space-first in the general case, with point-thinking as a special case for the situations where the space collapses to a point.

A single concrete case makes the taxonomy land. Consider the **harmonic oscillator**: a mass on a spring, displaced and released. The point-first question is "where is the mass at time \( t \)?" — and the answer is a point. But the space-first question is "what is the phase portrait of the system?" — and the answer is a two-dimensional space with a stable fixed point at the origin and closed orbits around it. The equations of motion describe a **flow** on that space, not a sequence of points. The physics is not in the position; it is in the geometry of the flow. A student who can only solve for the position has learned a special case. A student who can describe the phase portrait has learned the general case, of which the position is one feature among many.

#### 3.5 What the error costs

The cost of the point-first pedagogy is not that students cannot find roots. It is that they cannot **describe** the spaces in which roots are features. They can solve for \( x \) but cannot say what kind of space \( x \) inhabits. They can compute the discriminant but cannot say what it tells you about the parabola. They can find the break-even point but cannot describe the region of viability. The grammar of extraction produces students who can extract points from spaces they cannot describe.

### 4. The Alternative Grammar: Conditional Prediction

#### 4.1 The schema

The alternative grammar is a grammar of **conditional prediction**. Its sentence form is:

> **Given** [conditions], **we predict** [outcome], **expressed as** [relationship], **interpreted as** [feature of the space], **with** [failure modes].

Every mathematical statement is a **conditional**. The equation \( h(t) = h_0 + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \) is not a fact about the world. It is a sentence that says: *if* gravity is constant, *if* air resistance is negligible, *if* the throw is vertical, *then* the height at time \( t \) is given by this expression. The conditions are not footnotes. They are part of the sentence.

The grammar has five components:

1. **Condition:** what must be true for the prediction to hold.
2. **Prediction:** what the relationship says will happen.
3. **Relationship:** the equation that expresses the prediction.
4. **Interpretation:** what the relationship means — both referentially and structurally.
5. **Failure:** when the condition fails, and what takes over.

The current grammar has only the third component. It presents the equation as if it were self-sufficient — as if the relationship were the whole of mathematics, and the conditions, interpretation, and failure were optional decorations. The alternative grammar makes the third component **depend** on the first, and the fourth and fifth **depend** on the second. The equation is the language in which the prediction is expressed, and the prediction is the reason the equation exists.

#### 4.2 The grammar is a schema, not a template

A **template** is a form you fill in after the work is done. A **schema** is the form the work takes from the start. The grammar here is a schema: an equation without stated conditions is not an equation waiting for conditions to be added; it is an incomplete mathematical statement.

A statement that gives the relationship but not the conditions is incomplete: it does not say when it applies. A statement that gives the prediction but not the interpretation is incomplete: it does not say what the prediction means. A statement that gives none of the failure modes is incomplete: it does not say when the model breaks.

The five parts are **constitutive, not decorative**. A student who has learned the grammar knows that an equation without conditions is not a complete mathematical sentence — it is a fragment.

#### 4.3 The grammar does not replace equations

Some readers will think the grammar replaces equations with prose. It does not. The equation is the third component — the language in which the relationship is expressed. What the grammar replaces is the **presentation** of the equation as self-sufficient. The equation remains; what changes is its role. It is a sentence inside a larger sentence, not the whole of mathematics.

#### 4.4 Interpretation has two layers

The word "interpretation" is used throughout and does two different things. **Referential** interpretation maps variables to real-world quantities: \( t \) is time, \( h \) is height. **Structural** interpretation names features of the space the relationship defines: roots, vertex, regions of sign, asymptotes, symmetries. Both are required. An equation with referents but no structure is a label; an equation with structure but no referents is a formal exercise. The grammar requires both.

#### 4.5 Scope: applied mathematics, not pure

The grammar as stated applies to mathematics that describes or predicts the world. It does not directly apply to pure mathematics, where the "conditions" are axiomatic and the "interpretation" is internal to the formal system. The scope claim is therefore: **the grammar reconstructs applied mathematics and mathematical modeling, not the whole of mathematics.**

A companion argument for pure mathematics is possible — the conditions are the axioms, the prediction is the theorem, the interpretation is the model-theoretic content, and the failure is the counterexample — but it is not developed here. The paper's claim is that the grammar is complete for applied mathematics, and that applied mathematics is the part of the curriculum where the point-first pedagogy's failure is most damaging and most visible.

#### 4.6 The use case comes first

The grammar begins with a **real-world question** and states the conditions under which an answer is possible. It sounds like this:

> "A ball is thrown upward at 20 meters per second from a height of 1.5 meters. Given that gravity is approximately constant at \( 9.8 \, \text{m/s}^2 \) near the Earth's surface, and given that air resistance is negligible for this speed and shape, we predict that the ball's height at time \( t \) is \( h(t) = 1.5 + 20t - 4.9t^2 \). Under these conditions, the ball reaches its peak at about 2.04 seconds and lands at about 4.2 seconds. If the ball is a feather, the model fails; if the throw is at an angle, the model needs a horizontal component."

The use case is the first sentence. The conditions are the second. The relationship is the third. The interpretation is the fourth. The failure mode is the fifth. The student does not need to defer the question "why?" because the answer is the **first sentence**.

### 5. Worked Examples

The examples below are a demonstration, not a syllabus. The claim is that the grammar generalizes; the claim is not that these examples exhaust the curriculum. Each example is structured by the five-part grammar, with failure as a structural component, not an add-on. The hard case — confidence intervals — is last.

#### 5.1 Projectile motion: prediction under natural law

**Condition:** Gravity is constant near Earth's surface; air resistance is negligible; the throw is vertical; initial height and velocity are known.

**Prediction:** \( h(t) = h_0 + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \). At \( t = 2 \) seconds, with \( h_0 = 1.5 \) m and \( v_0 = 20 \) m/s, the ball is at \( h(2) = 1.5 + 40 - 19.6 = 21.9 \) meters.

**Relationship:** A downward-opening parabola in \( t \).

**Interpretation:** Referential: \( t \) is time, \( h \) is height. Structural: the vertex is the maximum height; the roots are the launch and landing times; the discriminant tells us whether the ball ever reaches a given height.

**Failure:** If the ball is a feather, air resistance is not negligible and the model fails. If the throw is at an angle, the model needs a horizontal component. If the ball is thrown on the Moon, \( g \) changes and the coefficients change. The equation is a **conditional description**, not a law of nature.

#### 5.2 Area and perimeter: description under resource constraint

**Condition:** The fence is 40 meters long; the pen is rectangular; all fence is used; the ground is flat and uniform.

**Prediction:** If one side is \( x \), the other is \( 20 - x \), and area is \( A(x) = x(20 - x) \). The maximum occurs at \( x = 10 \), a square of side 10, with area 100 square meters.

**Relationship:** A downward-opening parabola in \( x \).

**Interpretation:** Referential: \( x \) is the side length, \( A \) is area. Structural: the vertex is the maximum area; the roots are the degenerate pens; the symmetry reflects the symmetry of the rectangle.

**Failure:** If the pen can be circular, the maximum area is larger — a circle of circumference 40 has area about 127 square meters. If the pen can use a river as one side, the optimal shape changes. The quadratic is a description of a trade-off under specific constraints, not a universal truth about pens.

#### 5.3 Break-even: prediction under market assumptions

**Condition:** Fixed costs \( F \); variable cost per unit \( c \); price per unit \( p \); all units sell at price \( p \).

**Prediction:** Profit is \( P(q) = (p - c)q - F \). Break-even is at \( q = F/(p - c) \).

**Relationship:** A linear function of \( q \).

**Interpretation:** Referential: \( q \) is quantity, \( P \) is profit. Structural: the slope is the unit margin; the intercept is the fixed cost; the break-even point is where the line crosses zero.

**Failure:** If price falls with quantity, the model becomes quadratic. If costs have thresholds, the model becomes piecewise. The linear model is a conditional description of a simplified market.

#### 5.4 Population growth: prediction under rate assumption

**Condition:** Constant per-capita growth rate \( r \); unlimited resources; no predation, disease, or migration.

**Prediction:** \( N(t) = N_0 e^{rt} \). The population doubles every \( \ln 2 / r \) years.

**Relationship:** An exponential function of \( t \).

**Interpretation:** Referential: \( N \) is population, \( t \) is time. Structural: the growth rate is the slope of the logarithm; the doubling time is a constant; the function has an asymptote at \( N = 0 \).

**Failure:** Resources are finite, so growth slows and the model becomes logistic: \( N(t) = K / (1 + ((K - N_0)/N_0)e^{-rt}) \). The exponential model is a conditional description of early-stage growth, valid only while resources appear unlimited.

#### 5.5 Deterrence: prediction under strategic interaction

**Condition:** The adversary is rational; the leader's utility is a trade-off between security and cost; the adversary's response is a function of the leader's investment; the interaction is modeled as a simultaneous-move game with observable payoffs.

**Prediction:** The optimal defense level is where the marginal security benefit equals the marginal cost, **given** the adversary's likely response. Formally, the leader chooses defense \( d \) to maximize \( U(d, r(d)) \), where \( r(d) \) is the adversary's response function.

**Relationship:** A constrained optimization problem with a trade-off surface. The first-order condition equates marginal benefit and marginal cost along the reaction curve.

**Interpretation:** Referential: the variables are defense spending and security outcomes. Structural: the "solution" is not a number but a **strategy** — a conditional plan that specifies what to do under different observable states of the world. The strategy is a **region** of the space, not a point: it is the set of defense levels that are robust across plausible adversary responses. A point solution (a single optimal \( d \)) exists only when the adversary's response function is known with certainty; when it is not, the solution is the region of \( d \) that performs adequately across the range of plausible responses.

**Failure:** If the adversary is not rational, the model fails — the reaction curve is not defined. If the adversary's response is non-linear, the trade-off surface changes and the first-order condition may have multiple solutions. If the payoffs are not observable, the game is one of incomplete information and the "solution" becomes a Bayesian equilibrium, which is a distribution over strategies, not a strategy. The model is a conditional description of a strategic interaction under specified assumptions, and the strategy-as-region interpretation is what survives when the point interpretation fails.

#### 5.6 Confidence intervals: prediction under uncertainty (the hard case)

The five examples so far are clean cases where the grammar's value is obvious. The hard case is statistics, where the point/space distinction is least intuitive and the grammar's value is easiest to miss.

**Condition:** A sample is drawn from a population; the sample is random; the population distribution is approximately normal, or the sample is large enough for the central limit theorem; the standard deviation is estimated from the sample.

**Prediction:** A 95% confidence interval covers the true mean in 95% of repeated samples. The interval is \( \bar{x} \pm 1.96 \cdot s/\sqrt{n} \).

**Relationship:** An interval whose width depends on the sample size and the sample standard deviation.

**Interpretation:** Referential: the interval is an estimate of the population mean. Structural: the "solution" is not a point estimate but a **region** with a coverage probability. The region shrinks as \( n \) grows; it widens as \( s \) grows; its coverage is a long-run property of the procedure, not a property of any single interval.

**Failure:** If the sample is not random, the coverage probability does not hold. If the population is heavy-tailed and the sample is small, the normal approximation fails. If the standard deviation is not estimated correctly, the interval is miscalibrated. And the failure the point-first pedagogy actually produces is this: the student reports "the mean is between 3.2 and 4.8" as if that were a fact about the population rather than a property of a procedure applied to a sample. The point-first student has extracted a region and mistaken it for a point — or worse, mistaken the region for a fact. The conditional-grammar student reports "under the assumptions of random sampling and approximate normality, the procedure covers the true mean in 95% of repeated samples; the interval for this sample is [3.2, 4.8]; if the sample is not random, the coverage claim does not hold." The difference between the two students is not the arithmetic. It is whether they know what the arithmetic is for.

### 6. The Foundational Effect

#### 6.1 What mathematics is taught to be

The current pedagogy teaches mathematics as a **closed grammar**: a system of rules for manipulating symbols, with the purpose deferred. The alternative teaches mathematics as an **open language**: a system for describing and predicting the world under stated conditions. The difference is not in the content — the same equations are taught — but in what the content is **for**. In the current pedagogy, the equation is the lesson. In the alternative, the equation is the **language** in which a prediction is expressed, and the prediction is the lesson.

#### 6.2 How failure is diagnosed

In the current pedagogy, a student who completes the problem set correctly is assumed to understand. In the alternative, a student who completes the problem set correctly but cannot state the conditions, interpret the relationship, or identify when the model fails has **not** understood. The alternative makes understanding **visible** — not just in the students who refuse to comply, but in the students who comply. The compliance mask is removed because compliance is no longer sufficient.

#### 6.3 How assessment changes: a worked example

The current assessment asks: "Solve for \( x \)." The alternative assessment asks questions that cannot be passed by compliance alone. Consider the following exam question for the projectile case:

> A ball is thrown upward at 15 m/s from a height of 2 m. (a) State the conditions under which the height of the ball is well-modeled by a quadratic function. (b) Write the relationship and predict the ball's maximum height. (c) Interpret the vertex, the roots, and the discriminant in terms of the ball's motion. (d) Identify one condition under which the model fails, and describe what happens to the ball when the condition fails.

A procedural exam would ask only (b). The conditional exam asks (a), (c), and (d), which cannot be answered by executing the method. A sample rubric for (a): "Award full credit if the student states that gravity must be approximately constant, air resistance must be negligible, and the throw must be vertical. Award partial credit if one condition is stated but not the others. Award no credit for restating the equation." The rubric makes the conditions the object of assessment, not the equation.

#### 6.4 How teacher training changes: a worked example

The current teacher training teaches **how to explain the method**. The alternative teaches **how to state the conditions**. A teacher-training exercise for the projectile case might ask the trainee to:

1. Write the relationship for projectile height.
2. List the conditions under which the relationship holds.
3. Identify a condition that fails in a common classroom example (e.g., a paper airplane, a balloon).
4. Describe what the student should observe when the condition fails.
5. Design a question that would reveal whether a student understands the conditions, not just the method.

A trainee who cannot complete steps 2 through 5 is not prepared to teach the grammar. The exercise is not about the physics; it is about the **conditional structure** of the physics. A teacher who has internalized the structure can apply it to any equation in the curriculum.

#### 6.5 What it costs

The transition to conditional-grammar pedagogy is not free. Textbooks would need rewriting; assessment would need redesign; teacher training would need to prioritize conditions over procedures. Assessment reform at the policy level is a precondition for the pedagogy change described here, not a consequence of it: a conditional-grammar curriculum assessed by a procedural exam would produce the same compliance mask in a new form.

The paper's claim is not that the transition is cheap. It is that the current pedagogy's costs — the compliance mask, the delayed failure, the students who leave, the students who comply without understanding — are higher than the transition's costs, and that they are currently invisible because the mask conceals them.

### 7. Objections and Replies

**Objection 1: "This is just applied math. Pure math needs abstraction."**

Reply: The alternative is not a rejection of abstraction. It is a **sequencing**. Abstraction is easier to learn when the student has seen what it abstracts **from**. The quadratic formula is more meaningful after the student has predicted projectile heights, mapped pen areas, and modeled break-even points. The abstraction is the **distillation** of those use cases, not a substitute for them. See §4.5 for the scope claim: the grammar reconstructs applied mathematics, not pure mathematics.

**Objection 2: "Students need to learn the syntax before they can use it."**

Reply: Students learn syntax **through** use. A child learns grammar by speaking, not by memorizing conjugation tables first. Mathematics is the same. The syntax of extraction is a **tool**; the use case is the **reason** the tool exists. Teaching the tool without the reason is like teaching someone to hold a hammer without showing them a nail.

**Objection 3: "Conditional emphasis makes math seem uncertain."**

Reply: Conditional emphasis makes math **honest**. Mathematics is certain **within its conditions**. The uncertainty is in the world, not in the math. Teaching students to state conditions is teaching them to think clearly about when a model applies and when it fails. This is not uncertainty; it is **precision**.

**Objection 4: "The gender claim is essentialist and dangerous."**

Reply: The claim is descriptive, not prescriptive. It describes average differences in the willingness to defer the question "why?", with large overlap and many exceptions. It is not a claim about capability, and it is not a reason to teach differently. The alternative pedagogy states the reason for all students, which removes the mask regardless of how the gender difference is explained.

**Objection 5: "This is infeasible at scale."**

Reply: The grammar does not require smaller classes or more resources. It requires a different sequencing of the same content. The same equation is taught; what changes is the order — condition before syntax, interpretation before application. The claim is not that the pedagogy is easy to implement. It is that the implementation cost is lower than the cost of the current pedagogy's masked failure, and that the sequencing change is available to any teacher who understands it.

**Objection 6: "Students still need procedural fluency."**

Reply: The grammar does not replace fluency; it motivates it. A student who understands what the quadratic formula **describes** is more likely to remember it than a student who has memorized it as a sequence of operations. The claim is not that procedure is unnecessary. It is that procedure without purpose produces the compliance mask, and that procedure **with** purpose produces both fluency and understanding.

**Objection 7: "What about pure mathematics? The grammar does not describe a proof."**

Reply: Correct, and the paper is scoped accordingly. The grammar reconstructs applied mathematics. A companion argument for pure mathematics is possible — conditions are axioms, prediction is theorem, interpretation is model-theoretic content, failure is counterexample — but it is not developed here. See §4.5.

---

### 8. Conclusion

The current pedagogy teaches mathematics as a closed grammar: solve for \( x \), find the answer, complete the problem set. It fails some students visibly — they refuse — and others silently — they comply without understanding. The silent failure is the more dangerous one, because it protects the pedagogy from critique. A classroom of completed problem sets looks like success even when no one knows what the language is for.

The alternative teaches mathematics as a language of conditional prediction: **given** these conditions, **we predict** this outcome, **expressed as** this relationship, **interpreted as** this feature of the space, **with** these failure modes. The use case comes first. The conditions are explicit. The prediction is the point. The equation is the language.

The grammar proposed here is a first step, not a finished framework. It needs empirical work — does conditional-grammar pedagogy produce measurable gains in transfer, retention, and conceptual understanding? It needs assessment design — what does a conditional-grammar exam measure that a procedural exam does not? The paper's claim is that these questions are worth asking, not that they have been answered.

The best mathematics student is not the one who solves for \( x \) fastest. The best mathematics student is the one who knows what the language is for.
